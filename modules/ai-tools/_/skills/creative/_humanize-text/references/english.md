# Humanize Text (English)

Remove the signals typical of text written or rewritten by AI in English.

The goal is not simply to make the text informal. It should read as if a real person wrote it, with rhythm, word choices, opinions and small natural irregularities appropriate to the context.

Preserve:

* the meaning;
* the facts;
* the intent;
* the appropriate level of formality;
* the author's existing personality.

Do not "polish" the text unnecessarily.

## Process

1. Read the whole text before changing anything.
2. Identify the common patterns of AI writing.
3. Rewrite only what sounds artificial, generic or over-organized.
4. Simplify sentences that seem written to sound intelligent.
5. Vary rhythm and sentence length.
6. Preserve the author's natural expressions, even when they are not perfectly formal.
7. Where appropriate, add a position, personality and specificity.
8. Do one last read asking: "Would a real person actually write it this way?"

---

# Core principle

Human writing is not necessarily informal.

A technical report can be human.
An academic paper can be human.
A two-line message can be human.

The problem is when the text reads as if it came out of a mold.

Avoid turning everything into casual English. The register should match who is writing and where the text will be published.

---

# 1. Artificial formality

## Look for

* it is important to note that
* it's worth noting that
* it should be noted that
* it is worth mentioning that
* it bears mentioning
* one must consider
* it is crucial to understand
* in this context
* in this regard
* from this perspective
* against this backdrop
* with this in mind
* that being said
* having said that

These expressions are not wrong, but they show up far too often in AI text.

## Fix

Go straight to the fact.

Example:

"It's worth noting that the system also lets you export the data."

→

"The system also lets you export the data."

# 2. Too many connectives

## Look for

* Furthermore
* Moreover
* Additionally
* In addition
* However
* Nevertheless
* Nonetheless
* On the other hand
* Thus
* Therefore
* Consequently
* Hence
* As such
* That said
* Ultimately
* Overall
* Finally

AI tends to put a connective at the start of nearly every paragraph.

## Fix

Drop the connective when the relationship between the sentences is already obvious.

Use connectives only when they genuinely help the reader.

# 5. Inflated importance

## Look for

* plays a crucial role
* plays a pivotal role
* represents a milestone
* marks a significant step
* underscores the importance
* highlights the need
* demonstrates the potential
* stands out as
* has emerged as
* is becoming increasingly relevant
* opens up new possibilities
* is a game changer
* is a testament to

## Fix

Describe what happened without trying to convince the reader that it matters.

# 6. The "not just X, but Y" structure

## Look for

* not just..., but also...
* it's not about..., it's about...
* more than just...
* goes beyond...
* isn't merely X; it's Y
* X isn't enough. Y is what matters.

Models reach for this constantly to manufacture depth.

## Fix

State the main point directly.

"It's not just about saving time, it's about transforming the way we work."

→

"The real gain is a change in how the work is organized."

Or simply:

"It saves time and changes how the work is organized."

---

# 7. Artificial rule of three

AI likes to build sequences of three elements even when there is no reason for it.

## Look for

"fast, simple, and efficient"

"clarity, efficiency, and innovation"

"people, process, and technology"

"secure, scalable, and reliable"

"faster, cheaper, and better"

## Fix

Keep only the items that actually add information.

Don't force lists of three.

---

# 8. Sentences that are too symmetrical

## Look for

Sentences like:

"Less complexity, more productivity."

"More control, less effort."

"Not just X. Y too."

"From planning to execution."

"From idea to reality."

"From concept to implementation."

"Simple in theory. Hard in practice."

These are standard marketing and AI structures.

## Fix

Use a construction specific to the subject.

---

# 9. Shallow analysis with participles

## Look for

* highlighting
* demonstrating
* showcasing
* reflecting
* ensuring
* allowing
* enabling
* providing
* contributing
* underscoring
* cementing
* making it easier to

Especially right after a comma.

Example:

"The system centralizes the data, providing greater efficiency."

## Fix

Explain the relationship concretely.

"The system centralizes the data, so the team doesn't have to check three separate tools."

---

# 10. Invented cause and effect

AI frequently turns two observations into a causal relationship with no evidence.

## Look for

"This demonstrates that..."

"This shows that..."

"This underscores..."

"Which highlights the importance of..."

## Fix

If the conclusion isn't demonstrable, cut it or make clear that it's your reading.

---

# 11. Vague attribution

## Look for

* experts say
* experts agree
* studies show
* research indicates
* many believe
* critics argue
* analysts note
* the consensus is
* it is widely believed

## Fix

Name who said it, which study showed it, or drop the attribution.

---

# 12. Restating the obvious

AI frequently makes a claim and then immediately explains what it just said.

Example:

"This reduces latency. In other words, responses reach the user faster."

If the second sentence adds nothing, cut it.

Watch especially for:

* in other words
* that is to say
* essentially
* basically
* what this means is
* put simply
* in practice
* at its core

These are natural in English, but they turn artificial when they show up constantly.

---

# 13. Repeated conclusion

Look for paragraphs where:

1. an idea is introduced;
2. the same idea is explained;
3. the same idea appears again as a conclusion.

Cut one of the versions.

Humans often just let the idea end.

---

# 14. Generic conclusions

## Look for

* in summary
* in conclusion
* to sum up
* ultimately
* at the end of the day
* looking ahead
* the future looks bright
* there's much more to explore
* the possibilities are endless
* this is just the beginning
* only time will tell
* one thing is clear

## Fix

End on the last concrete point in the text.

Not every piece needs a closing paragraph.

---

# 15. Artificial rhetorical questions

## Look for

"So what does this mean?"

"But why does this matter?"

"So what's the problem?"

"The answer?"

"But is that really the case?"

"Sound familiar?"

"Here's the thing."

Used repeatedly, these give the text the shape of an AI-generated script.

## Fix

Turn the question into a statement, or just carry on with the argument.

---

# 16. Over-organization

AI text tends to organize everything into:

* an introduction;
* three or four arguments;
* a conclusion;
* lists;
* subheadings;
* a closing summary.

Not every text needs that structure.

If two paragraphs work better than six sections, use two paragraphs.

If one sentence settles it, don't build a list.

---

# 17. Artificial lists

## Look for

* lists that try to be exhaustive;
* every item roughly the same length;
* every item starting with a verb;
* exactly three or five items for no reason;
* a small conclusion after each item.

## Fix

Mix prose and lists where it makes sense.

Don't try to cover every possibility.

---

# 18. Generic headings

## Look for

* Final Thoughts
* Key Benefits
* Key Takeaways
* Challenges and Opportunities
* What to Expect
* Why It Matters
* Looking Ahead
* Conclusion
* Next Steps
* The Bottom Line
* Introduction

## Fix

Use a specific heading or no heading at all.

---

# 19. Synonyms used only to avoid repetition

AI frequently renames a thing in every sentence.

Example:

"the app"
"the platform"
"the solution"
"the tool"
"the system"
"the offering"

If they all refer to the same thing, this makes the text more artificial and less precise.

## Fix

Repeat the same term when it is the right term.

Natural repetition beats forced synonyms.

---

# 20. Excessively neutral tone

Human writing is allowed to have an opinion.

Where appropriate:

* say that something is odd;
* say that something is inconvenient;
* admit that a solution is good but has a problem;
* say which alternative you'd pick;
* acknowledge uncertainty without turning it into bureaucratic language.

Example:

"This approach presents certain limitations."

→

"It works, but I'd avoid it on large projects because it gets hard to maintain."

Don't invent opinions when the text is supposed to be neutral.

---

# 21. Emotional precision

Avoid vague words like:

* concerning
* interesting
* impressive
* surprising
* notable
* compelling

when you can explain why instead.

"This is concerning."

→

"What bothers me here is that a single error now takes down every service."

---

# 22. Rhythm that is too uniform

AI tends to produce sentences of similar length and structure.

Mix short sentences with more developed ones.

This can be perfectly natural.

So can a longer sentence, when there is something that genuinely needs explaining and breaking it into four pieces would only get in the way.

Don't try to create rhythm with dramatic short sentences all the way through.

---

# 23. Short sentences that are too dramatic

Another AI pattern:

"It's simple."

"But there's a problem."

"And it's big."

"Really big."

"That's the point."

Use this sparingly.

Don't turn ordinary writing into copywriting.

---

# 24. Lecturing tone

## Look for

* it's important to understand that
* remember that
* keep in mind that
* bear in mind that
* it's essential to grasp
* first and foremost
* before we go further, we need to understand

## Fix

# 25. Filler

Replace where possible:

"in order to"
→ "to"

"due to the fact that"
→ "because"

"at this point in time"
→ "now"

"has the ability to"
→ "can"

"it is important to note that"
→ delete

"it's worth noting that"
→ delete

"with regard to"
→ "about"

"in terms of"
→ often deletable entirely

"in the event that"
→ "if"

"prior to"
→ "before"

"the vast majority of"
→ "most"

"a wide range of"
→ name the actual range

"utilize"
→ "use"

"facilitate"
→ "help"

# 26. Excessive hedging

## Look for

* can potentially
* may possibly
* tends to
* somewhat
* to some extent
* relatively
* arguably
* seemingly
* it could be argued that
* in many cases
* generally speaking

## Fix

If there is uncertainty, express it once.

"Probably X."

is better than:

"It could be argued that X may potentially occur in certain situations."

---

# 27. Register too formal for the context

Not every situation calls for editorial English.

In conversations, posts, forums and personal writing, these are natural:

* contractions — "don't", "it's", "you'll";
* starting a sentence with "But" or "And";
* fragments;
* parentheses;
* small repetitions;
* incomplete sentences used deliberately;
* plain words — "use" over "utilize", "help" over "facilitate".

Don't introduce artificial errors.

Just stop automatically correcting natural features of the author's voice.

# 31. "It depends" followed by a full taxonomy

AI frequently answers a simple question by inventing categories:

"It depends on three main factors..."

Then it produces a small academic classification of the subject.

If a simple answer is enough, give the simple answer first.

# 32. False comprehensiveness

## Look for

"from X to Y"

"whether X, Y, or Z"

"ranging from..."

"across various contexts"

"for all kinds of users"

"in a wide variety of scenarios"

"everything from ... to ..."

## Fix

# 34. Pretty but empty generalizations

## Look for

"Technology continues to evolve rapidly."

"The world is more connected than ever."

"Innovation has never been more important."

"We live in an era of constant change."

"In today's fast-paced world..."

"More and more companies are looking for efficient solutions."

# 35. Overuse of "can"

Models use "can" to avoid committing:

"This can help improve..."

"This can enable..."

"This can contribute to..."

When the relationship is direct, be direct.

"This reduces..."

"This lets you..."

When the uncertainty is real, keep it.

---

# 36. Em dashes and punctuation tics

The em dash is the single most recognizable punctuation signal of AI English. Models use it as a default connector, often more than once per paragraph.

## Look for

* an em dash where a comma, a period or parentheses would do
* two or more em dashes in the same paragraph
* a colon used to set up a dramatic reveal — "The result: chaos."
* semicolons in casual writing
* "scare quotes" for emphasis rather than quotation
* ellipses used for suspense...

## Fix

Keep the em dashes you would actually have typed. Turn most of the rest into commas, parentheses, or a full stop.

An occasional em dash is fine. A paragraph with three is a tell.

---

# 37. Formatting tics

Heavy formatting is a signal on its own, independent of the words.

## Look for

* a bolded phrase in every bullet
* Headings With Every Word Capitalized
* emoji as section markers
* every list item shaped as "**Term** — definition"
* bullets nested three levels deep
* a bolded lead-in at the start of every paragraph
* a horizontal rule between every section

## Fix

Bold something when it needs to stand out from what surrounds it. If everything is bold, nothing is.

Prose is allowed to be prose.

---

# Add personality

Once the artificial patterns are gone, check that the text hasn't gone sterile.

Where it fits:

* keep real opinions;
* keep humor;
* keep small useful digressions;
* allow drier sentences;
* allow a personal observation;
* use first person where it makes sense;
* keep the author's specific word choices;
* preserve some asymmetry.

Don't try to make every sentence memorable.

Human writing also contains completely ordinary sentences.

# Final test

Before delivering, ask:

1. Is there a sentence that exists only to introduce another sentence?
2. Is there an adjective that adds no information?
3. Is there a conclusion repeating what was already said?
4. Are there too many connectives?
5. Are there lists that could be one sentence?
6. Is the text more organized than the context calls for?
7. Does the vocabulary sound more formal than the author normally uses?
8. Are there generic opinions where a specific observation belongs?
9. Does any sentence read like LinkedIn, corporate marketing, or a college admissions essay for no reason?
10. Are the em dashes and bold text doing real work?
11. Would a real person write this unprompted?

If the answer to any of them is "no", adjust.

---

# Output

Deliver the rewritten text first.

Don't explain each change.

If asked, follow up with a short summary of the main changes.

Don't say the text was "humanized", "optimized" or "polished".
